2The physics of a turn

A turn absorbs an inbound delay as long as the aircraft still has its minimum turn time on the ground. The model is a hinge,

outbound_delay = a + beta * max(0, inbound_delay - (scheduled_turn - tau))

where tau is the effective minimum turn time (it includes crew, cleaning and boarding) and beta the pass-through rate beyond it. Fitted per carrier on $train_window by profiling tau over a 1-minute grid, with 95% CIs from a bootstrap that resamples whole days. The estimates are remarkably sharp: tau ranges from $tau_range minutes (regional carriers turn fastest) and beta from $beta_range: past the threshold, delay passes through one-for-one.

3Measuring the contagion

To count how much later delay one late departure creates, take clean starts: legs whose aircraft was ready (first leg of the chain, or inbound on time) but which still left late, so the delay is primary by construction. Sum the arrival delay of every later leg of that aircraft, and subtract the same sum for on-time clean starts of the same carrier, on the same day, in the same part of the day, with the same number of legs left to fly. Matching on the day removes weather and ATC conditions; matching on legs left removes exposure.

4Where to put the buffer

Padding turns costs aircraft time, so the question is where a limited budget does the most good. For Southwest ($turns_per_day turns a day), a decision is an extra number of scheduled minutes for every turn at a station in a given departure hour ($cells station-hour cells). The delay recursion along every aircraft chain uses the fitted tau and beta, with primary delays and en-route changes taken from the data, so that zero buffer replays each observed day exactly. A sample-average-approximation LP over $scenario_days training days chooses the buffers that minimise expected arrival delay under a daily budget ($lp_size, solved with HiGHS). Policies are then scored on $test_days held-out days.

Delay avoided on held-out days against the buffer actually scheduled on those days. Bands: 95% day-bootstrap CI.

With $budget buffer minutes a day, the LP avoids $lp_avoided $lp_avoided_ci minutes of arrival delay per held-out day, against $uniform_avoided for uniform padding and $greedy_avoided for padding the most-delayed turns (paired difference to uniform: $greedy_gain $greedy_gain_ci). That is $lp_per_min minutes avoided per buffer minute, against $uniform_per_min and $greedy_per_min. Paired over days, the LP's edge over uniform is $lp_gain $lp_gain_ci minutes per day.

Does the LP overfit its scenarios?

An LP fitted on a handful of days learns their noise. Re-solving on independent random draws of 10 to 90 training days shows the in-sample edge over uniform padding shrinking from $size_train_small to $size_train_large as scenarios are added, while the held-out edge stays between $size_test_range: the structure it learns (where turns are tight relative to the delay that reaches them) carries over to unseen days.

5Method notes and limitations

  • Clock. BTS times are local. Every leg is converted to UTC with its airport's IANA zone; the arrival date is resolved against the published block time, which handles overnight, date-line and DST legs (tested on hand-made cases).
  • Time split. Parameters, LP buffers and heuristic rankings use $train_window only; every performance number is on $test_window.
  • Contagion is observational. The matched comparison removes day-level conditions and exposure, not aircraft-level confounders. For delays under 15 minutes the estimate is noticeably higher, a sign that small slips also flag tighter schedules.
  • Buffer model. A buffer minute is modelled as slack only: it does not change primary delays, crew legality, gate conflicts or the published schedule's commercial value. The LP is a ranking device for where slack pays, not a full schedule re-optimisation.
  • Coverage. Chains break at cancellations, diversions, tail swaps and ground times over 5 hours. Hawaiian reports under Alaska from January 2026 and Spirit stops reporting in May 2026.
