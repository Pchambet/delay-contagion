1$reactionary_share of delay comes from the previous flight

The BTS codes every arrival delay of 15 minutes or more into five causes. One of them, late-arriving aircraft, is not a new problem: it is an earlier delay riding the aircraft into its next leg. Over $window it is $reactionary_share of all cause-coded delay minutes, and it builds through the day: $reactionary_6am minutes per departing flight at 6:00, $reactionary_peak by $reactionary_peak_hour. Across carriers the share runs from $cause_share_range; cause codes are self-reported, so that spread needs care.

2The physics of a turn

A turn absorbs an inbound delay as long as the aircraft still has its minimum turn time on the ground. The model is a hinge,

outbound_delay = a + β · max(0, inbound_delay − (scheduled_turn − τ))

where τ is the effective minimum turn time (it includes crew, cleaning and boarding) and β the pass-through rate beyond it. Fitted per carrier on $train_window by profiling τ over a 1-minute grid, with 95% CIs from a bootstrap that resamples whole days. τ ranges from $tau_range minutes and β from $beta_range: past the threshold, delay passes through about one-for-one. The held-out bins show a rounded knee rather than a sharp corner. τ intervals narrower than about a minute reflect the grid, not certainty to the second.

Dots: held-out months ($test_window), mean outbound delay in 5-minute bins of ground time left. Lines: hinge fitted on training months. Click legend entries to compare carriers.

Out of sample, the hinge predicts outbound delay with a mean absolute error of $mae_hinge minutes. Baselines fitted on the same training turns: a constant ($mae_constant), a linear model in inbound delay ($mae_linear), the same with the scheduled turn as a second regressor ($mae_linear_slack), and the hinge with τ pinned at zero ($mae_hinge_tau0). Slack only helps once it enters through the threshold. A gradient-boosted model on inbound delay, scheduled turn, carrier and hour does better, at $mae_gbm (better on $gbm_wins carriers with held-out data); the hinge keeps $hinge_share_of_gbm of its improvement over the slack-aware linear model with two interpretable parameters, and it keeps the decision model linear. $filter_excluded of held-out turns fall outside the delay window used for fitting and scoring (inbound −90 to 600, outbound −60 to 600 minutes).

Is β causal? Several carriers and hubs have β slightly above 1 with intervals that exclude 1. A turn cannot amplify delay, so this points to common shocks: weather or ATC at the station delays the inbound and the outbound's own departure together. The decision model reads β as the effect of slack; section 4 checks how much that matters.

3Measuring the contagion

To count how much later delay one late departure creates, take clean starts: legs whose aircraft was ready (first leg of the chain, or inbound on time) but which still left late. Sum the arrival delay of every later leg of that aircraft, and subtract the same sum for on-time clean starts of the same carrier, on the same day, in the same part of the day, with the same number of legs left to fly (capped at 4+). Matching on the day removes carrier-wide, day-level conditions, not local weather or a ground stop at one airport; matching on legs left removes exposure.

How robust is the $multiplier?

"Clean start" is an approximation of primary: $coded_share_events of these events still carry a late-aircraft cause code ($coded_share_min of their minutes), for instance after a tail swap. The headline survives every check below. Adding the origin airport to the match key (local weather, ground stops) leaves $mult_origin_events events and gives $mult_origin; exact legs left gives $mult_exact_legs; dropping late-aircraft-coded legs gives $mult_no_coded. Origin matching also explains the odd first bin: slips of 1 to 14 minutes look more contagious ($small_slip_mult) because they cluster at congested airports; matched within the airport they drop to $small_slip_origin. Legs left is counted on the realised chain, after cancellations and swaps the delay may itself cause, so it is partly a post-treatment quantity.

4Where to put the buffer

Padding turns costs aircraft time, so the question is where a limited budget does the most good. For $lp_carrier_name ($turns_per_day turns a day in training), a decision is an extra number of scheduled minutes for every turn at a station in a given departure hour ($cells station-hour cells). The delay recursion along every aircraft chain uses the fitted τ and β, with primary delays and en-route changes taken from the data, so that zero buffer replays each observed day exactly. A sample-average-approximation LP over all $scenario_days training days chooses the buffers that minimise expected arrival delay under a daily budget ($lp_size, solved with HiGHS). Policies are then scored on $test_days held-out days (the chart at the top).

What "delay avoided" means. Arrival delay is measured against the padded published schedule, the DOT on-time definition airlines are judged on. A buffer never makes an aircraft earlier on the clock: it moves that departure and every later one of the aircraft's day back in the timetable, and absorbs the late inbound inside that slack. Because the shift carries down the chain, the same buffer minutes count as avoided on each later leg that would have been late, which is how one buffer minute can avoid more than one minute of delay. Padding buys predictability, not speed.

The budget is set on training-day traffic; summer days fly more turns ($turns_per_day_test a day), so at a $budget min/day budget the LP spends $lp_spend and uniform padding $uniform_spend min/day on held-out days. Per buffer minute, LP-placed buffer avoids $lp_per_min minutes of arrival delay against $uniform_per_min for uniform padding: $lp_vs_uniform as much. In total the LP avoids $lp_avoided $lp_avoided_ci minutes per held-out day, $lp_share_of_baseline of the $baseline_delay delay minutes of an average held-out day; uniform padding interpolated at the same spend would avoid $uniform_at_lp_spend.

Two heuristics show how much of that needs an optimiser. Padding the turns with the most observed propagated delay does worse than uniform padding ($greedy_per_min per buffer minute; paired difference to uniform $greedy_gain $greedy_gain_ci min/day): those turns are late because their inbound is late, not because their slack is short. A marginal-value greedy that ranks cells by the delay a full buffer would remove there, one cell at a time, gets much closer: $marginal_per_min per buffer minute, so the LP's joint placement adds a further $lp_vs_marginal_pct per buffer minute. Rounding the LP's buffers to 5-minute schedule steps costs little ($rounded_per_min per buffer minute at $rounded_spend min/day). Fractional uniform padding is $uniform_seconds seconds per turn, which no planner would publish; its implementable version, 5 minutes on randomly chosen station-hours, gets $uniform_step_per_min.

Most of fractional uniform padding's A15 edge is a threshold artefact: a fraction of a minute on every turn moves arrivals of exactly 15 minutes under the line, and the 5-minute version scores $ontime_uniform_step. An airline that cares about the A15 count should optimise that objective instead.

Does the evaluation flatter the LP?

The held-out replay uses the same hinge the LP optimised against: out of sample in days, but in-model. Re-scoring the same buffers with the replay's τ shifted by ±5 and ±10 minutes, or with β = 0.8, moves the absolute minutes a lot ($sens_lp_range min/day for the LP) but keeps its per-minute advantage over uniform padding in the range $sens_ratio_range. The ranking is robust; the absolute numbers depend on the model.

5Method notes and limitations

  • Clock. BTS times are local. Every leg is converted to UTC with its airport's IANA zone (first occurrence in the repeated fall-back hour); the arrival date is resolved against the published block time, which handles overnight, date-line and DST legs (tested on hand-made cases).
  • Time split. Parameters, hubs, LP buffers and heuristic rankings use $train_window only; every performance number is on $test_window.
  • Contagion is observational. The matched comparison removes carrier-wide day-level conditions and exposure, not aircraft-level confounders; the robustness table shows how far the estimate moves under stricter matching.
  • Buffer model. A buffer minute is modelled as slack only: it does not change primary delays, crew legality, gate conflicts or the commercial value of a departure time. The LP is a ranking device for where slack pays, not a full schedule re-optimisation; a budget-neutral re-allocation of existing slack would be needed to gain clock time.
  • Uncertainty. CIs on held-out policy results resample whole weeks; hinge and contagion CIs resample days. All are conditional on the model.
  • Coverage. Chains break at cancellations, diversions, tail swaps and ground times over 5 hours. $carriers_without_test reports under Alaska from January 2026 (no held-out turns; $n_carriers_test carriers are scored) and Spirit stops reporting in May 2026 ($spirit_test_turns held-out turns).
